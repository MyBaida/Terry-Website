Customer Service · Real Client Story

Two Days as a Customer: What I Found When I Stopped Being the Consultant

A few years ago, when I was invited to run a series of workshops for the management and staff of a hospitality company outside Accra, I decided to begin with due diligence of a different kind.

I spent two days inside the organization as a customer. The exercise was deliberately covert. Only the General Manager and the HR Manager knew why I was there. To everyone else, I was just another guest walking through the doors.

Those two days revealed situational realities that no briefing note or management report could have captured. By the time the workshops began, the conversations were grounded not in theory, but in the everyday experiences of the organization itself.

"Effective training often begins long before the training room. It begins with understanding the real environment in which people work."

The Full Story

I started with a question that I'm still answering.

Why do capable people and promising organizations consistently fall short of their potential? Two decades, serveral books, and hundreds of consulting engagements later, I am still working on the answer — from Accra, for Africa, and for the world.

I began my career in market research, where I learned the discipline of separating observation from assumption — a habit that has shaped every piece of consulting work I have done since. From there, I moved into strategy, communications, human capital, and leadership development, building a practice that eventually became Terry Mante Exchange.

Along the way, I started writing. First books, then articles — sixteen of them now, across leadership, personal development, governance, personal finance, and, most recently, fiction. The writing and the consulting feed each other: every idea I put in a book has been tested in a boardroom, and every engagement I take on teaches me something I eventually write about.

I am the Principal Consultant at Terry Mante Exchange and a weekly columnist in the Business & Financial Times. My work has been cited by researchers and PhD candidates in Australia, Ghana, India, Nigeria, the United Kingdom, and the United States.

Featured · May 2026

Professionalism Shouldn't Depend on Fear

There's a pattern I've observed both as an employer and as a management consultant working closely with SMEs: calm, respectful leadership is often mistaken for weakness.

The moment a boss chooses dignity over drama, some employees quietly recalibrate their behaviour — not upward, but downward. Deadlines become elastic. Instructions are treated as suggestions. Basic professional courtesies start to slip.

The real issue is a cultural gap in how professionalism is internalized. Respect should not be conditional on a boss's temperament. A healthy workplace shouldn't require hostility to function.
